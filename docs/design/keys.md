# Keys and Certificates

## Purpose

This note records how a connection's keys and certificates are kept, generated, rotated and read (SSO-2.5 to SSO-2.7,
SSO-2.11, SSO-2.12, SSO-5.9): the service provider's own key pairs (SAML signing and encryption, OIDC
`private_key_jwt`), each moving through `next`, `current` and `retiring`, and the identity provider signing certificates
a SAML connection trusts as a set. Which signature and encryption algorithms each key serves is decided with the SAML
and OIDC drivers, not here.

## Invariants

- Every key and certificate is one row of `sso_keys` (`keys.table`), on the storage connection, written through
  `Models\ConnectionKey` so it is keyed by a ULID and stamped by `Contracts\Clock`. A row holds its use, its state, its
  key's type and size, the certificate and public key (canonical PEM), the SHA-256 fingerprint of the certificate's
  DER, the certificate's validity, when it was made current and until when a retiring key is accepted. The unique index
  on `connection_id`, `use` and `fingerprint` keeps one row per key or certificate of a use in a connection.
- A row's use (`Keys\Enums\KeyUse`) says what it is and what may hold it:

  | Use                | Whose                                                   | Protocol | Types allowed | Freshness              |
  |--------------------|---------------------------------------------------------|----------|---------------|------------------------|
  | `sp_signing`       | The service provider's, signing `AuthnRequest`s         | `saml2`  | RSA           | `sso.keys.rotate`      |
  | `sp_encryption`    | The service provider's, decrypting encrypted assertions | `saml2`  | RSA           | `sso.keys.rotate`      |
  | `client_assertion` | The relying party's, signing `private_key_jwt`          | `oidc`   | RSA, EC       | `sso.keys.rotate`      |
  | `idp_signing`      | The identity provider's, trusted by the connection      | `saml2`  | RSA           | `sso.connection.trust` |
  | `metadata_signing` | A metadata signing anchor, trusted by the connection    | `saml2`  | RSA           | `sso.connection.trust` |

  Every SAML use is RSA alone while SAML stays on PHP 8.3 and saml2 v6 (`saml-library.md`, condition 8): a generated key
  of another type for `sp_signing` or `sp_encryption` is refused (`key_type`, `not_allowed`), and a certificate of
  another type given for `idp_signing` or `metadata_signing` is refused (`certificate`, `unsupported`). ECDSA arrives
  when the PHP floor moves to 8.5 and saml2 v7 is stable.

  A `client_assertion` key must also fit the connection's `client_assertion_algorithm` when it pins one
  (`ClientAssertionKeys`): one that does not is refused (`key_type`, `not_allowed`), before it is generated and again
  under the connection's lock. RSA signs with `PS256` or `RS256`, P-256 with `ES256` (`oidc-driver.md`).

- The service provider's own key pairs are generated by the package (`Keys\KeyGenerator`) with PHP's OpenSSL
  extension alone, so they depend on no SAML or JOSE library: RSA of `sso.keys.rsa_bits` (3072 by default, or 4096),
  or EC on P-256 (`KeySettings::EC_CURVE`). Each comes with a self-signed certificate whose subject and issuer are the
  connection's id alone, signed with SHA-256, with a random positive serial, `basicConstraints = critical,CA:FALSE`,
  valid from the moment it is signed for `sso.keys.certificate_days`. Every OpenSSL option is passed explicitly and the
  package ships no `openssl.cnf`: OpenSSL builds a certificate request only from a configuration file, so each
  generation writes a minimal one (no default subject field) to a temporary file of its own and removes it, whatever
  happens; the system's file, whose default subject fields would otherwise land in the certificate, is never read.
- A private key is encrypted through `Contracts\SecretEncrypter` before it is written, and is never returned by a
  helper (SSO-2.5). Between generation and encryption it is held as a `Secrets\Secret` inside `Keys\GeneratedKey`, which
  shows `[redacted]` in a dump and refuses to be serialised. The key ring decrypts it only for the call that signs or
  decrypts with it, again as a `Secret`. A trusted certificate has no private key.
- States and transitions of an own key (`Keys\Enums\KeyState`); at most one `current` and one `next` of a use per
  connection:

  | Operation       | From                   | To                                                                     | Reported                                  |
  |-----------------|------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
  | `addNext()`     | (none of the use)      | `current`, made current now                                            | `KeyAdded`, then `KeyMadeCurrent`         |
  | `addNext()`     | a `current`, no `next` | `next`                                                                 | `KeyAdded`                                |
  | `makeCurrent()` | `next`                 | `current`; the old `current` becomes `retiring` until the overlap ends | `KeyMadeCurrent`, naming the key replaced |
  | `retire()`      | `next` or `retiring`   | removed                                                                | `KeyRetired`                              |

  Anything else is `key_state_conflict`: a second `next`, making current a key that is not `next`, retiring the
  `current` key, or acting on a trusted certificate with an own-key operation.
- The overlap: a key replaced as current becomes `retiring` with `retiring_until` set to the moment it was replaced
  plus `sso.keys.overlap_seconds` (a week by default; 0 to 90 days). Decryption accepts the current encryption key and
  each retiring one whose `retiring_until` is after the clock's now, the most recently current first; an overlap of 0
  ends as the key is replaced. A retiring key stays a row, and still decrypts nothing, until it is retired.
- Publishing: metadata and a JWKS carry the current key and then the next one (`KeyRing::published()`, SSO-5.9), so an
  identity provider learns a key before it signs or encrypts with it.
- Trusted certificates are a set (SSO-2.6): up to `KeyService::MAX_TRUSTED_CERTIFICATES` (10) per connection, each
  judged before it is trusted by `Keys\CertificateInspector`, every violation at the setting `certificate`:

  | Given                                                        | Code                  |
  |--------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------|
  | More than 16384 bytes                                        | `too_long`            |
  | Anything holding `PRIVATE KEY` (checked before parsing)      | `private_key_refused` |
  | Not exactly one PEM-encoded X.509 certificate                | `invalid_format`      |
  | RSA under 2048 bits, or a curve but P-256, P-384 or P-521    | `weak_key`            |
  | Any other kind of key (DSA, Ed25519, ...)                    | `not_allowed`         |
  | A certificate the connection already trusts                  | `duplicate`           |
  | An eleventh certificate                                      | `too_many`            |
  | A connection whose protocol is not `saml2`, or a key not RSA | `unsupported`         |

- Expiry is reported, never enforced (SSO-2.6): a certificate's dates are read and stored, an expired certificate is
  trusted, and the key ring offers every trusted certificate whatever its dates. `KeySummary::isExpiredAt()` and
  `expiresWithin()` answer the report; `Keys\ExpiryReport` and `KeyNearingExpiry` warn of it (`event-model.md`).
- Every helper (`Keys\KeyService`) follows the management helpers' order (see `outcomes.md`): find the tenant's
  connection, and the key, through `Connections\ConnectionFinder` on the write connection; validate what was given;
  check the key's state; ask `Freshness\FreshnessGate` with the use's operation; generate the key, when one is added;
  then, in one transaction on the storage connection, read the connection again with `findForUpdate()` (which
  serialises a connection's rotations), check the state again, write, and report through
  `Administration\AdministrationEvents`. A key or certificate is always read constrained to its connection's id. The
  keys read again under the connection's lock are read locked too: a plain read there would answer from the snapshot
  of a transaction the caller opened (MySQL's `REPEATABLE READ`), which may predate a rotation committed before the
  lock was taken.
- Results are `Keys\KeyResult` values, status `Keys\Enums\KeyStatus`: `added`, `made_current`, `retired`, `trusted`
  and `distrusted` carry the `KeySummary` of the key acted on (as it was, for a removal), and `made_current` the key it
  replaced; `refused` carries `freshness_required`, `connection_not_found`, `key_not_found` or `key_state_conflict`;
  `invalid` carries `settings_invalid` and its violations. A refusal writes nothing and reports nothing. `keys()`
  answers `null` for a connection it did not find, and every row otherwise, by use, then current, next, retiring and
  none, then oldest first.
- `KeyService` and `KeyRing` hold no state between calls and are resolved by autowiring; nothing is bound for them.

## Success Path

1. An administrator adds the connection's first signing key: `addNext()` generates it and stores it as `current`.
2. When it is time to rotate, `addNext()` stores a new key as `next`; metadata now carries both.
3. Once the identity provider has the new key, `makeCurrent()` makes it current; the old one is `retiring` and, for
   encryption, still decrypts until the overlap ends.
4. After the overlap, `retire()` removes the old key.
5. For the identity provider's own rotation, `trustCertificate()` adds its new certificate alongside the old one, and
   `distrustCertificate()` removes the old one once the provider has switched.

## Failure / Edge Cases

- A connection of another tenant is `connection_not_found`, exactly as an unknown one; a key of another connection, or
  an id that cannot be a key's, is `key_not_found`; `distrustCertificate()` given an own key is `key_not_found`.
- A state that changed between the first read and the transaction is `key_state_conflict` (a key made current or a
  `next` added meanwhile); a key removed meanwhile is `key_not_found`; a certificate trusted meanwhile is `duplicate`.
- A stale caller is `freshness_required`, with nothing generated, written or reported.
- Removing the last trusted certificate is allowed: the SAML driver then finds the connection incomplete, so no sign-in
  runs through it until another is trusted.
- A connection's first metadata signing anchor brings it to hold a stale deadline and, unless `metadata_refresh` is
  `false`, to refresh from its `metadata_url`, and clears what was stamped before, in the same transaction; removing its
  last ends both (`Metadata\SamlRefreshRule`, `metadata-refresh.md`).
- Distrusting a certificate, and trusting a metadata signing anchor, is a trust change (SSO-2.13): in its
  transaction, under the connection's lock, it voids the connection's unfinished authorisation requests and unredeemed
  handoff codes and raises its trust version (`Connections\InFlightVoider`; see
  [connection-lifecycle.md](connection-lifecycle.md)), so none completes or is redeemed against the certificates it was
  started under. Trusting another identity provider certificate is additive and voids nothing, so a provider's next
  certificate can be trusted before it switches; it still asks `sso.connection.trust`. Rotating one of the service
  provider's own keys (`addNext()`, `makeCurrent()`, `retire()`) keeps what subjects mean and voids nothing.
- A malformed key setting fails closed with an `InvalidArgumentException` naming its key (and `[keys]` reports it at
  boot); an OpenSSL failure is a `RuntimeException` naming the step, never the key, with OpenSSL's error queue cleared;
  a `SecretEncrypter` that cannot encrypt, a ciphertext that cannot be decrypted, and a database error propagate.
- `KeyRing::privateKey()` given a key of another connection, a trusted certificate or an unknown id throws a
  `LogicException`: the caller's mistake, never a refusal.

## Change Triggers

- A use, a state, a transition, or what each use may hold.
- How a key is generated: its types, sizes, certificate fields or validity, or a library taking over generation.
- What is encrypted, or where a private key may be decrypted.
- The certificate checks, their codes, or the cap.
- Whether expiry is enforced anywhere.
- The overlap, or what decryption accepts.
- A helper's order, its reads and writes, or what it reports.
